Timber Properties & Behavior

Physical and mechanical characteristics of structural wood

💧

Moisture Content (MC)

Most influential factor affecting timber strength, stiffness, and dimensional stability. Fiber saturation point (FSP) is typically 25–30% MC.

MC (%) = (m_wet − m_dry)/m_dry × 100
  • Below FSP: strength increases as MC decreases
  • Above FSP: strength remains constant
  • Service MC: 8–19% depending on climate
  • Shrinkage/swelling occurs only below FSP
📐

Anisotropic Behavior

Timber has different properties along three orthogonal directions: longitudinal (L), radial (R), and tangential (T). Strength is highest parallel to grain.

  • L: Parallel to grain — highest strength
  • R: Radial to growth rings
  • T: Tangential to growth rings — most shrinkage
  • E_L ≈ 10–20 × E_R ≈ 20–30 × E_T
⚡

Strength Properties

Timber is strong in tension and compression parallel to grain but weak perpendicular to grain and in shear.

  • Bending (f_b): 5–50 MPa depending on species/grade
  • Compression ∥ (f_c): 10–60 MPa
  • Tension ∥ (f_t): 5–40 MPa
  • Shear ∥ (f_v): 1–8 MPa
  • Modulus of Elasticity (E): 5–20 GPa
🔥

Fire Resistance

Timber chars at a predictable rate (~0.65 mm/min for softwoods). The uncharred core retains structural capacity, allowing engineered fire design.

  • Char rate β ≈ 0.65 mm/min (softwood)
  • Residual section carries load after charring
  • Large timber members perform better than steel in fire
  • Fire-retardant treatments available
🛡️

Durability & Decay

Biological attack (fungi, insects, marine borers) requires moisture, oxygen, suitable temperature, and food source.

  • Natural durability classes: 1 (very durable) to 5 (not durable)
  • Heartwood > sapwood in natural resistance
  • Preservative treatment: CCA, ACQ, LOSP, creosote
  • Design life categories: 1 (temporary) to 5 (permanent)
🏗️

Engineered Wood Products

Manufactured timber products offer improved consistency, larger spans, and higher strength than sawn timber.

  • Glulam: Laminated timber beams/columns
  • CLT: Cross-laminated timber panels
  • LVL: Laminated veneer lumber
  • OSB/PLY: Panel products for sheathing
  • I-Joists: Flange-web engineered joists

Timber Engineering Notes

Comprehensive study material for design and practice

📘 Timber Beam Design — Key Points

  • Bending Stress: σ = M·y/I = M/Z. Allowable bending stress is reduced by size factor, load duration factor, moisture factor, and temperature factor. For rectangular sections: Z = bd²/6.
  • Deflection Limits: Typically span/250 to span/360 for total load; span/500 for live load in sensitive applications. Long-term deflection under sustained load can be 2–3× immediate elastic deflection due to creep.
  • Lateral Torsional Buckling: Deep narrow beams are susceptible to lateral buckling. Lateral restraint spacing must satisfy code limits (e.g., 90×b for continuous restraint, or check slenderness λ_rel,m).
  • Notches & Holes: Avoid notches on tension side. If unavoidable, reduce section properties and check stress concentration. Never notch middle third of span. Holes should be drilled, not punched, with minimum 75 mm from edges.
  • Load Duration Factor (k_mod / C_D): Timber strength increases for short-duration loads. Permanent: 0.6; Long-term: 0.7; Medium-term: 0.8; Short-term: 0.9; Instantaneous: 1.1 (Eurocode/NDS values vary slightly).

📗 Column & Compression Design — Key Points

  • Slenderness Ratio: λ = L_e/r, where L_e = effective length (depends on end conditions) and r = √(I/A) = radius of gyration. For rectangular timber: r = b/√12 about strong axis, d/√12 about weak axis.
  • Euler Buckling: P_cr = π²EI/L_e². Applicable when λ > λ_limit (typically > 100 for timber). For intermediate slenderness, use Perry-Robertson or code-specific column curves.
  • Combined Bending & Axial: Interaction equation: (σ_c/f_c)² + σ_b/f_b ≤ 1.0 (simplified). Check both axes for biaxial bending.
  • Effective Length Factors: Pinned-pinned: 1.0; Fixed-fixed: 0.65; Fixed-pinned: 0.8; Fixed-free: 2.1. Bracing must prevent lateral displacement and rotation as assumed.

📙 Connection Design — Key Points

  • Nailed Connections: Capacity depends on nail diameter, timber density, penetration depth, and number of shear planes. Minimum end distance = 15d, edge distance = 5d, spacing = 15d (parallel) and 5d (perpendicular). Pre-boring required for dense hardwoods.
  • Bolted Connections: Design checks: embedment strength of timber, yield moment of bolt, and Johansen's yield theory (European Yield Model). Multiple bolts require reduction factor due to uneven load distribution.
  • Dowel & Connector Plates: Ring connectors, toothed plates, and split rings increase bearing area and reduce slip. Gang-nail plates used extensively in truss fabrication.
  • Glued Connections: Adhesives (PUR, PRF, MUF, epoxy) create rigid, high-strength joints. Finger joints and scarf joints restore full section capacity. Critical for glulam and CLT fabrication.

📕 Engineered Timber & Sustainability — Key Points

  • Glulam: Layers ≤ 45 mm thick, grain parallel, bonded with moisture-resistant adhesive. Curved and tapered beams possible. Strength class GL24h, GL28h, GL32h (characteristic bending strength in MPa).
  • CLT: Odd number of orthogonally bonded layers (3, 5, 7, 9). Acts as plate in two directions. Used for floors, walls, and roofs in mass timber construction. Fire rating up to 90 minutes achievable with adequate thickness.
  • Sustainability: Timber sequesters ~1 tonne CO₂ per m³. FSC/PEFC certification ensures responsible sourcing. Life cycle analysis (LCA) favors timber over concrete/steel for low embodied carbon.
  • Acoustics & Vibration: Timber floors require careful design to meet vibration criteria (span/depth ratios, concrete topping, or proprietary acoustic systems). Fundamental frequency f₁ should exceed 8 Hz to avoid resonance.

Softwoods vs. Hardwoods

Common structural timber species and their applications

🌲

Pine / Spruce

Softwood, most common structural timber. Good strength-to-weight, easy to work. Used for framing, trusses, formwork.

🌳

Douglas Fir

High-strength softwood. Excellent stiffness and natural durability. Preferred for heavy timber beams and glulam.

🌿

Larch

Durable softwood with high resin content. Good for external applications, cladding, and marine structures.

🍂

Oak / Teak

Hardwoods with high density and natural durability. Used for bridges, marine piles, and heritage restoration.

🪵

Bamboo

Grass with timber-like properties. Rapidly renewable. Used in laminated products and scaffolding (Asia).

🔧

Treated Timber

Softwood preserved with CCA, ACQ, or boron. Essential for ground contact and exposed outdoor structures.

Structural Applications

Timber systems in civil engineering construction

🏠

Light-Frame Construction

Platform framing using dimensional lumber (2×4, 2×6, 2×8). Shear walls with OSB/plywood sheathing resist lateral loads. Most common residential system in North America.

  • Stud walls at 400 or 600 mm centers
  • Platform vs. balloon framing
  • Braced panels and shear walls
🏛️

Heavy Timber & Post-Beam

Large sawn or glulam members with steel connectors. Exposed timber creates aesthetic value. Common in commercial and institutional buildings.

  • Glulam beams up to 50+ m spans
  • Arch and portal frame configurations
  • Traditional mortise-tenon or steel connections
🔺

Timber Trusses

Prefabricated trusses using gang-nail plates or bolted steel gussets. Efficient for roofs and bridges. Warren, Pratt, Howe, and fan truss configurations.

  • Roof trusses: spans 5–20 m typical
  • Bridge trusses: covered bridges, pedestrian
  • Space trusses for long-span roofs
🌉

Timber Bridges

Short to medium span bridges using glulam girders, stress-laminated decks, or truss systems. Popular for rural and pedestrian applications.

  • Glulam girder bridges: up to 30 m
  • Stress-laminated timber decks
  • Nail-laminated and dowel-laminated decks
🏢

Mass Timber Buildings

CLT, glulam, and LVL used for multi-story construction. Fire design relies on charring protection of structural cores.

  • CLT floor and wall panels
  • Platform construction: 5–12+ stories
  • Hybrid timber-concrete systems
🚧

Formwork & Temporary

Timber is the traditional and still widely used material for concrete formwork due to ease of cutting, nailing, and cost.

  • Plywood-faced timber frames
  • Beam formwork with timber joists
  • Tubular steel-timber composite systems

Timber Connections

Methods of joining timber members in structural systems

🔩 Nailed Connections
Most common in light-frame construction. Round wire nails, ring-shank nails, and helical-thread nails provide withdrawal resistance.
  • End distance ≥ 15d
  • Edge distance ≥ 5d
  • Spacing ≥ 15d (parallel)
  • Pre-bore dense species
🔧 Bolted Connections
Used in heavy timber for moment-resisting and shear connections. Steel dowel action transfers load through bearing and bending.
  • Diameter: 12–30 mm typical
  • Washers prevent crushing
  • Multiple bolts: group reduction
  • Johansen yield theory
⚙️ Connectors & Plates
Ring connectors, toothed plates, split rings, and shear plates increase bearing area in heavy connections.
  • Split ring: 64–165 mm diameter
  • Shear plate: timber-to-steel
  • Gang-nail plates: truss fabrication
  • Concealed connectors for aesthetics
🔗 Glued Connections
Structural adhesives create rigid, high-capacity joints. Essential for engineered wood products.
  • Finger joints in glulam production
  • Scarf joints: angled grain transition
  • Epoxy for hardwood connections
  • PUR/PRF/MUF for mass timber
➕ Steel Brackets
Proprietary hold-downs, post bases, joist hangers, and angle brackets for light-frame and heavy timber.
  • Joist hangers: face/ top mount
  • Hold-downs: resist overturning
  • Post bases: uplift & lateral
  • Concealed knife plates
🪵 Traditional Joinery
Mortise-tenon, dovetail, scarf, and lap joints in traditional timber framing. Often combined with hardwood pegs.
  • Mortise-tenon: beam-column
  • Bridle joint: corner connections
  • Scarf joint: splice long members
  • Dovetail: tensile resistance

Formula Quick Reference

Essential equations for timber structural design

📐 Bending & Deflection

Section Modulus (Rectangular)

Z = b·d² / 6

b = width, d = depth perpendicular to neutral axis

Moment of Inertia

I = b·d³ / 12

Second moment of area for rectangular section

Maximum Bending Stress

σ_m = M / Z ≤ f_m,d

M = applied moment; f_m,d = design bending strength

Maximum Deflection (UDL)

δ = 5·w·L⁴ / (384·E·I)

Simply supported beam, uniformly distributed load

🏗️ Compression & Stability

Slenderness Ratio

λ = L_e / r    r = √(I/A)

L_e = effective length, r = radius of gyration

Euler Buckling Load

P_cr = π²·E·I / L_e²

Elastic critical load for pin-ended column

Combined Stress (Interaction)

(σ_c/f_c)² + σ_m/f_m ≤ 1.0

Biaxial bending + axial compression check

Fire — Residual Section

d_res = d₀ − 2·β·t

β = charring rate (~0.65 mm/min), t = fire duration

🔗 Connection Design

Embedment Strength (Eurocode)

f_h,k = 0.082·ρ_k·d^(-0.3) (N/mm²)

ρ_k in kg/m³, d = fastener diameter in mm

Yield Moment of Fastener

M_y,Rk = 0.3·f_u,k·d^2.6

f_u,k = ultimate tensile strength of fastener

Nail Lateral Capacity (Simplified)

F = k·d²·√(ρ_k)

k depends on joint geometry and number of shear planes

Number of Fasteners

n_req = F_d / (k_mod·F_Rk / γ_M)

Apply modification factors and material partial factor
